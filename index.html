SmartMoney

An AI financial advisor that isn't allowed to do the math

Shipped · solo build, deployed with CI/CD

React · Tailwind · Java Spring Boot · Groq API · LLaMA · Railway

You give it your income, expenses and goals. It gives you back a personalized budget and an optimal debt-payoff plan, computed from your actual numbers rather than pulled from a template.

Financial advice is either generic to the point of being useless, or expensive enough that the people who need it most can't afford it. SmartMoney was my attempt at the third option.

I integrated Groq's LLaMA model over REST, built a React and Tailwind frontend with interactive debt calculators and payment-timeline visualizations, and deployed a Spring Boot backend to Railway behind a full CI/CD pipeline.

Plain English

Avalanche and snowball are the two standard ways to pay off several debts at once. Avalanche attacks the highest interest rate first and costs you least overall; snowball clears the smallest balance first and is easier to stick with.

Tool-calling means the AI can't invent a number. When it needs one, it calls my function and reports what came back.

The important architectural decision was that the model does not do arithmetic. I wrote the financial library myself — avalanche and snowball amortization, a financial health score, goal-feasibility projections — and the model reaches those functions through a tool-calling interface. The advice in the chat and the numbers in the interface come from the same tested code, so they cannot disagree with each other.

What I learned. The hardest problem wasn't engineering, it was making the output feel human. Early versions were technically correct and tone-deaf — telling a student with $800 a month in income to "reduce discretionary spending," acknowledging nothing about their actual life. The quality of an AI output is almost entirely determined by the quality of what you hand it, and that changed how I approach AI products generally.

The failure I overcame. I built the frontend and backend in parallel instead of designing the API contract first, and paid for it in integration friction I had personally created. I also underestimated how much edge-case handling financial data demands, because people's finances don't fit neatly into forms.

The sharpest failure came later. In live testing, the advisor produced a savings projection that was simply invented. Tracing it took longer than building the feature, and the cause turned out to be three unrelated bugs stacked on top of each other: an incomplete prompt constraint, a tool schema that instructed the model to omit a required parameter, and internal tool names leaking into user-facing text. I verified each fix against the live API rather than against mocks, since mocks would have cheerfully confirmed the broken behavior. That's why the safety constraints are now enforced by tests that fail the build if a guardrail is removed, instead of by a prompt I hope holds.

ReturnFlow

Automating the worst part of buying things online

Shipped · full stack — mobile, API, schema

React Native · Flask · SQLite · SQLAlchemy

A mobile-first platform that runs an e-commerce return from the moment a customer starts it to the moment it's resolved — replacing a process most retailers still run on email threads and spreadsheets.

Returns are one of the most broken experiences in e-commerce. Customers face confusing policies, manual tracking, and no visibility into where their return actually is. I built the React Native frontend, designed the REST API in Flask, and architected the relational database with SQLite and SQLAlchemy.

Plain English

A schema is the blueprint of a database: which tables exist, what each stores, how they connect. Getting it wrong late is expensive, because everything built on top has to move too.

The failure I overcame. I designed the initial schema around the simple case, and real-world data never is the simple case. Partial returns, split orders and status reversals all broke the model, and I had to refactor the schema completely mid-project. It cost me a week.

It also permanently changed how I work. I now treat the schema as the most important design decision in any project, and the one I am least willing to rush.

Ride Rite

Zero-commission ride-hailing for New York's yellow taxis

Built & researched · app, pricing model, regulatory strategy, patent brief

Expo · React Native · Supabase · Stripe

Instead of taking 25–30% of every fare the way Uber and Lyft do, drivers pay a flat monthly subscription and keep the entire fare.

It's aimed at NYC medallion taxi drivers, who watched their passenger volume move to apps they were largely locked out of. The idea came from reading about Namma Yatri, an Indian ride-hailing platform built on exactly that model. The question was whether it could work in a city that regulates taxis heavily — so most of my work went into the regulation, not the app.

Plain English

An E-Hail Provider License lets an app dispatch taxis that already exist. An FHV Base License means operating your own fleet. Different regulators, different obligations — picking the wrong one changes the entire company.

Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank is a 2014 Supreme Court decision that made abstract software and business-method ideas unpatentable unless claimed as a concrete technical implementation.

I spent months on the ground the idea would have to stand on: the difference between the Taxi and Limousine Commission's E-Hail Provider License and an FHV Base License, the Flex Fare program that lets a licensed app quote an upfront price instead of running the meter, and the wheelchair-accessible vehicle requirements attached to licensing.

I decided to pursue the E-Hail license first and to distribute through the NYC Taxi Workers Alliance rather than recruiting drivers one at a time. I deliberately chose not to implement surge pricing, because the entire pitch is predictability. I designed a two-part pricing model combining a fair-pay floor with a discount pegged relative to a predicted Uber fare, and wrote a full end-to-end product description for a patent attorney — specifying the algorithm concretely rather than describing the idea abstractly, which is the distinction Alice turns on.

Then I built the app itself from scratch in Expo and React Native with Supabase and Stripe, having never written a mobile app before.

What I learned. I assumed the app was the hard part. It isn't. A competitor, Empower, built a working zero-commission product and ran it in New York without the license — and that regulatory exposure is why it is not a real competitor today. The permission to dispatch is the product.

Plain English

Row-level security denies access to every row in a database by default, until you write rules for who may read or write each one. Turning it on without writing those rules locks out everyone — quietly.

The smaller failure. I turned on Supabase row-level security and wrote no policies, so reads and writes failed silently and nothing ever raised an error. It ran, and it didn't work, and those are not the same thing.

Station 43

A recruitment site rewritten from the recruit's point of view

Shipped · +50% applications, 20+ new volunteers

HTML · CSS · Adobe Suite · UX writing

Our volunteer recruitment website was written for us, not for the person considering applying. I rebuilt it around them, and applications rose 50%.

It assumed familiarity, buried the information that mattered, and put friction in front of someone applying for the first time. I redesigned it end to end: restructured the layout, rewrote the messaging from the prospective volunteer's perspective, and stripped every unnecessary step out of the application itself. We brought in more than 20 new volunteers.

What I learned. This taught me more about product thinking than anything I've built since. Most sites fail not because the design is bad, but because they're written from the organization's point of view instead of the user's. The shift from "what do we want to say" to "what does this person need to feel, and do next" is something I now apply to everything I build.

Klio Care

Learning to be told no, on someone else's payroll

Not code · ran the sales function, without a formal title

Lead lists · pitch materials · outreach scripts · field work in NJ, San Antonio, El Paso

At a healthcare startup building home-health referral and order-signing workflows, I built the provider lead lists, wrote the pitch and the outreach scripts, and did the field outreach myself.

I contacted more than 500 doctors' offices and onboarded roughly 40. The conversion rate is the lesson. Most of the job is hearing no, adjusting the script, and going again — and I would much rather have learned that on someone else's payroll than on my own company's.

Built with real intention, technology doesn't just automate a process. It reshapes how people connect with institutions, and with each other.

Liquid

A mobile wallet for College Park

Co-founded with Pranav Puranik · incorporated in Maryland, approved by Stripe

React Native · Stripe Connect · Maryland LLC

Students load money into a campus wallet and get a discount at partner restaurants they could never get paying with a card — because we can offer merchants economics the card networks can't.

Restaurants near campus lose two to three percent of every transaction to interchange, and have almost no direct channel to students. Liquid gives them a cheaper rail and access to exactly the customers they want.

Plain English

Interchange is the fee card networks take out of every card payment, typically 2–3%, charged to the merchant. For a deli on thin margins, that's real money.

T+2 settlement means funds from a payment land in the merchant's bank two business days later, not instantly.

The deposit mechanic is what makes the discount real rather than promotional. A restaurant's biggest objection to discounting is uncertainty — they're cutting margin on the hope that volume follows. When a student has already loaded money into the ecosystem, the restaurant isn't discounting on hope. It's discounting against spend that has already been committed. That's why partners sign.

We've incorporated in Maryland, been approved by Stripe, built the app in React Native, and launched live payment processing.

  • Marathon Deli
  • Pizza Kingdom
  • Craving Hour Halal

The hard part is not the app. Holding other people's money is a regulated activity. I had to work out whether our stored-value balances make us a money transmitter under Maryland law — which would require licensing we cannot realistically obtain as students — and I took that question directly to the Maryland Office of Financial Regulation rather than guessing. The answer shaped the product: how balances are held, how funds settle, and what we are legally permitted to call the thing.

The payment flow runs on Stripe Connect using separate charges and transfers, which means understanding a T+2 settlement window and what it does to a restaurant's cash flow before promising anything in a sales meeting. I also had to navigate Apple's App Store rules on payments, where the exemption we rely on is narrow enough that getting it wrong means getting pulled.

The failure worth naming. Early on I counted verbal commitments from owners as signed partners, and built a launch timeline on top of them. An enthusiastic yes in a conversation is worth close to nothing until there is a signed merchant agreement and a configured terminal. We rebuilt the pipeline so that signatures and live transactions are the only milestones that count.

What's next. Student-to-student transfers and delivery — so the wallet becomes the default way money moves on campus, rather than a discount card.

Feedback routing

Catching the complaint before it becomes a one-star review

Concept · B2B SaaS for independent restaurants

Text every customer after their meal. Send the happy ones to Google, and route the unhappy ones to the manager who can actually fix it — before their frustration goes public.

The reviews that end up on Google Maps almost never represent the average customer experience. Unhappy customers are motivated to write; happy ones forget. Independent restaurants end up held hostage by a feedback mechanism they don't control.

So: a customer finishes their meal and leaves. A few minutes later they get a text asking them to rate the experience from one to ten. Seven or above, they're thanked and sent a Google review link, capturing positive sentiment while it's still fresh. Below seven, they're asked what went wrong and given the server's name, the manager's contact, and corporate where relevant.

The Google link still goes out either way, as Google's policy requires — but a customer who has already been heard is far less likely to publish a damaging public review. Their frustration has somewhere to go that isn't the internet.

51 percent of negative reviews cite poor service specifically. This routes that feedback to the person who can act on it, and raises the public rating at the same time.

SmartMoney, expanded

From self-reported numbers to real transactions

Next build · consumer fintech

The prototype is a solid foundation, but it runs on numbers people type in — and almost nobody knows what they actually spend in a month.

Plain English

Plaid is the service that securely connects an app to a user's bank account so it can read transactions — the plumbing behind most consumer finance apps.

Connecting through Plaid grounds the advice in actual transactions. An advisor that can see real spending, identify patterns and give specific guidance is useful in a way a form-based tool never is.

The long-term version is a financial co-pilot for young people. Not a robo-advisor for people who already have investments, but a tool for someone trying to get out of debt, build an emergency fund, or make rent on a first apartment. That market is enormous and almost entirely unserved for anyone who can't afford a financial advisor.

Next step is the Plaid integration, and a more conversational interface that removes the friction of setup.
